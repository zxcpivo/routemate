_Recorded: 2026-11-07_

## Source and evidence status

These notes summarize the two interview transcripts Gleb supplied in chat. Participants are identified as Person 1 and Person 2; no names were supplied. The interview dates were not separately confirmed. This is self-reported customer evidence, not independently verified fares, expenses, travel times, or observed use of Routemate.

The team has two supplied interviews: one driver and one transit passenger. Earlier simulated interviews are not customer evidence. Neither participant has booked or paid for Routemate in the evidence provided. See [product.md](product.md) for the agreed product direction.

The team's concept is **rides arranged beforehand, preferably with the same driver on recurring routes and days**. This clarification came from Gleb after checking with a teammate. Product choices are separate from evidence that customers will use the product.

### Useful participant wording

> “Unless they're on the way, then it would be fine.”

> “Ten max?”

The second quote was given in response to a question about acceptable detour time. Do not present it as willingness to carry a particular stranger every day.

### What this supports

This driver has already carried familiar passengers, and route convenience affects the experience. It gives a concrete reason to show added travel time and let drivers choose their detour limit. Familiarity appears relevant to how much inconvenience and payment they would consider, but willingness to accept an unfamiliar recurring passenger remains untested.

### Still unknown

- Available seats, willingness to commit to particular pickups, and acceptable waiting time.
- Required contribution, preferred payment arrangement, and willingness to share with an unfamiliar person.
- What happens when either party changes plans or cancels.
- Whether enough passenger trips overlap with this route and schedule.

### Actual disruption and workaround

- Recalled a recent problem/delay with the second bus, described as technical.
- Took an Uber because they did not want to wait and reported paying about $18.
- The question referred to the recent week, but the exact incident date, delay duration, and whether the Uber prevented lateness were not established.
- This is one reported paid backup trip. It does not establish weekly Uber use or a monthly emergency-ride total.

### Existing lifts

- Sometimes receives a lift to work from another person when their work schedules line up.
- Still returns home by bus on those occasions.
- Identified mismatched schedules as an inconvenience for one or both people.
- Mentioned the other driver's “4:00” start time. AM/PM and the relationship to the participant's 7 a.m. start were unclear; do not reconstruct a precise shared schedule from this passage.
- Payment wording was ambiguous. They appear to have offered a contribution, but whether money was actually paid, how much, and whether the driver requested it are unresolved.

### Reaction after the app pitch

After Gleb described planned, shared rides and cost contributions, the participant expressed strong interest if someone travelled toward work at a suitable time. They mentioned less stress, more sleep, and possible money savings, and indicated they might spend more to gain sleep.

This is **hypothetical interest after a pitch**, not a booking, payment, accepted price, or commitment to ride with an unfamiliar driver. No matched car journey duration was measured. The interviewer suggested faster and safer travel; neither benefit was established by this interview.

### What this supports

There is a concrete example of extra spending after a transit disruption and existing behaviour accepting lifts. Schedule compatibility limits those lifts. This supports investigating both available transport at a fixed start time and coordination between people making compatible journeys. It does not establish which issue is the dominant root cause across the target market.

This participant suggests a candidate subgroup: **campus residents with fixed early work shifts and long transit journeys**. Student status is suggested by the setting but was not explicitly confirmed in the transcript. Age, budget, and lack of car access were also not established.

### Specific follow-up questions

1. “Which intersection is closest to your workplace?”
2. “Which four days do you usually work?”
3. “The last time you got a lift to work, what time did you leave and arrive?”
4. “Have you actually paid that driver anything?” If yes: “How much?”
5. “What happens at work when you arrive after 7?”
6. “What did you pay for transit last month?”
7. “When you said the other driver starts at 4, did you mean 4 a.m. or 4 p.m.?”

## Comparison and findings

| Observation | What the evidence supports | What remains unproven |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Person 1 already gives friends occasional lifts | Sharing a car is existing behaviour for this driver | Accepting unfamiliar passengers or a regular paid commitment |
| Person 1 dislikes going out of the way | Pickup/drop-off convenience matters to this driver | A ten-minute default suits the wider driver population |
| Person 2 paid about $18 after a bus problem | One real, self-reported example of paying for a backup | How often this happens or what recurring carpool price they would accept |
| Person 2 gets lifts only when schedules align | Compatible times affect access to existing rides | Enough matching drivers exist for the required days and arrival |
| Person 2 still buses home after receiving a lift to work | Outbound and return journeys need separate coverage | A morning match will also solve the return trip |
| Person 2 expressed strong interest after the pitch | A reason to seek a concrete follow-up | Adoption, repeat use, willingness to pay, or trust in a stranger |

**These two participants are not an established match.** Person 1 travels Markham to Keele around 9 a.m.; Person 2 needs campus to Etobicoke before 7 a.m. Their routes and times differ. Their travel durations cannot be used as a direct car-versus-transit comparison or as evidence of Routemate's time savings.

## How to use this information

### Product and prototype priorities to test

- Show recurring days and required arrival times for both sides.
- Show estimated added pickup/drop-off time and the driver's chosen limit.
- Treat outward and return journeys separately.
- Let both sides agree on a specific contribution in advance; interviews have not established the amount.
- Clarify confirmation, waiting, and cancellation expectations. A backup driver is still a proposed capability, not proven available supply.

These are design priorities informed by the accounts, not validated feature demand or instructions to expand the agreed build scope in [product.md](product.md).

### Demo

Person 2 supplies a concrete passenger situation: four workdays, a 7 a.m. arrival requirement, and a two-bus commute. Unknown exact days and locations must remain marked as unknown or be explicitly labelled synthetic if filled for a demo. A compatible driver is also hypothetical until found. Person 1 supplies a separate driver example, not the missing match.

### Pitch wording supported by the interviews

> We interviewed one driver and one transit passenger. The driver described inconvenient pickups. The passenger reported spending about $18 on Uber after a bus delay and receiving occasional lifts only when schedules matched. We're testing whether planned, recurring carpools can help commuters with compatible routes and times.

Describe the sample as two interviews. Do not present it as a representative survey or quote the simulated interviews as additional real participants. No measured improvement in safety, emissions, punctuality, cost, or journey duration has been established.

### Next useful evidence

- Another campus resident with a fixed early work shift, to check whether Person 2's problems recur in the proposed subgroup.
- A driver already travelling from near campus toward Person 2's workplace early enough for a 7 a.m. arrival.
- Actual overlap in days, times, routes, acceptable detour, and contribution, followed by a concrete agreement if both sides choose to proceed.
- Evidence of how often delays or paid backup trips happen. One recalled incident is insufficient to establish the team's weekly-pain criterion.

## Interview quality and limitations

- Some questions and reactions were leading or evaluative, including descriptions of neighbourhoods, suggestions of being attacked, and judgments that costs were reasonable. No specific crime or harassment incident was reported by either participant.
- The product pitch suggested benefits before the participant's reaction. Keep that reaction separate from their earlier descriptions of actual behaviour.
- In future interviews, ask what happened, what the person did, and what it cost. Let the participant judge the impact on their own time and budget.
- For payment, separate the facts: did money change hands, how much, and how was the amount agreed? An offer to contribute is not a completed payment.
- Two interviews provide useful cases and questions to pursue; they do not settle the ICP or establish marketplace supply.
