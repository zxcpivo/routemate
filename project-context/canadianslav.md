# Carpooling project context

## UNHack challenge

**Connected, Safe & Future-Ready Communities**

> How might we help make transportation safer, more affordable, and more available for fixed schedule commuters (work/school commuters)?

The challenge invites us to explore mobility, infrastructure, digital access, emergency response, civic participation, and responsible technology. It connects to UN Sustainable Development Goals 9 (resilient infrastructure and innovation), 11 (inclusive, safe, resilient, and sustainable communities), 16 (peaceful and inclusive societies and accountable institutions), and 17 (partnerships).

This file captures the first of five planned idea-planning Q&As, which focused on sharpening the problem statement. The customer segments, route, and product details are working hypotheses.

## Original idea

- **Driver-led scheduling.** Drivers already going to work or school enter their destination, required arrival time, and recurring commute days. Passengers can join if pickup and drop-off are close to the driver's existing route. The initial idea was to keep added travel time around 10 minutes. The acceptable detour is still open.
- **Cost sharing.** Riders contribute toward fuel and vehicle costs. The idea is to agree on the amount in advance and make payment predictable so drivers spend less time coordinating or following up. Riders could save money when the ride replaces an on-demand trip.
- **Trust and safety.** A recurring arrangement could let people ride with someone familiar. Route tracking and arrival check-ins are possible features. Comfort riding alone, trust in an unfamiliar driver, and concerns about crowded transit are interview topics.
- **Other potential benefits.** The original idea included meeting people and optional matching by age, gender, or interests. Treat these as secondary until interviews show which preferences affect ride choices. The emissions benefit depends on which trips a shared ride replaces and how much extra driving it adds.

## First customer to interview

Start with hourly workers in Brampton who lack regular access to a car and need to transfer between buses to reach a fixed early shift at a Pearson-area workplace. Recruit around one workplace and one shift window so the trips are comparable. This is a proposed starting corridor. Suburban students with fixed class schedules are a separate group to explore.

On the driver side, speak with people who already drive a similar route at a similar time. Test whether finding compatible riders, committing to pickup times, and agreeing on contributions are problems they have actually encountered.

## Five Whys

1. **Why do workers leave early, arrive late, or pay extra?** A missed connection can put them past a fixed shift start.
2. **Why is a missed connection costly?** The next bus may come too late to get them to work on time.
3. **Why do they rely on the transfer?** Their neighborhood and workplace may lack a direct transit option at the hour they need it.
4. **Why is switching modes difficult?** A car or informal lift may be unavailable, and repeat ride-hail fares can strain their budget.
5. **Why does this keep happening?** The shift time stays fixed, and affordable options on that trip leave little room to recover from a delay.

The root cause to test is a mismatch between a fixed arrival time and the affordable transportation available for that trip.

## Problem statements

**Broad.** We believe suburban GTA commuters without regular access to a car struggle to find reliable, affordable transportation when work or school has a fixed start time. Today it is a trade-off between leaving extra early, risking lateness, and paying more for a ride.

**Focused.** We believe hourly workers in the suburban GTA without regular access to a car struggle to reach fixed-start shifts on time when their commute requires a bus transfer. Today it is a recurring strain on their time and budget because they build in extra travel time or pay for a backup ride.

**Sharp (recommended for interviews).** We believe hourly workers in Brampton without regular access to a car struggle to reach early shifts at a Pearson-area workplace on time and within budget when they have to transfer between buses. Today it is a choice between leaving early enough to absorb a missed connection, risking a late arrival, or paying for a backup ride.

## Assumptions to test

1. Workers regularly lose meaningful time, arrive late, or spend money they had not budgeted.
2. Passengers' past choices suggest they would use an affordable alternative if they felt comfortable taking it. Find out when familiarity or safety concerns affected a real decision.
3. Enough drivers already make compatible trips on overlapping days and times, and riders can afford a contribution that makes a recurring pickup worthwhile for drivers.

## Interview questions

1. **Passenger.** Walk me through how you got to work and home last week. What time did you leave and arrive, and where did you wait or transfer?
2. **Passenger.** Tell me about the last commute that went differently from what you planned, if there was one. What did you do, and what did it cost in time, money, or missed work?
3. **Passenger.** Tell me about the last time you considered getting a ride from someone. What did you choose, and what mattered in that decision?
4. **Driver.** Walk me through your drives to and from work last week. Which days, routes, and times were consistent, and what changed?
5. **Driver.** Tell me about the last time you arranged, offered, or turned down a ride on your commute. What influenced your decision? If you shared the ride, how did pickup and expenses work?
